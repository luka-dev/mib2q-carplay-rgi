Abstract of a thesis at the University of Miami.

No. of pages in text. (70)



   Blind stereo-to-surround upmixing aims to convert stereo audio into a multichan-

nel surround mix without access to original multitrack recordings. Traditional up-

mixing approaches rely on Primary-Ambient Extraction (PAE) using digital signal

processing (DSP) techniques, which often suffer from limited generalization and spa-

tial artifacts. This work proposes a deep learning-based alternative, integrating high-

complexity state-of-the-art (SOTA) music source separation (MSS) models to enhance

ambient signal extraction and stereo decorrelation.

   We evaluate the efficacy of SOTA MSS architectures for separating primary and

ambient components for improved spatial and spectral coherence. The selected deep

neural network (DNN) architecture is trained on a custom dataset constructed from

open-source music data and assessed using both objective SDR and SI-SDR metrics

and a controlled participant listening study.

   Experimental results from objective metrics and a controlled listening study show

that MSS models can be effectively adapted for ambient signal extraction, outper-

forming prior deep learning approaches to the separation task and matching tradi-

tional DSP-based upmixing systems. These findings validate our proposed system

as a viable and perceptually effective solution for automatic blind stereo-to-surround

upmixing that utilizes deep learning throughout the entire processing chain.

CHAPTER 1

Introduction


1.1     Context and Motivations

   The evolution of audio consumption from mono to stereo, and now to surround

sound, reflects a continuous pursuit of increasingly immersive and realistic listening

experiences [12]. Despite these advancements, a significant gap remains in making

high-quality surround sound content as accessible and widespread as stereo audio.

   The typical workflow for a mixing engineer to create surround sound content

requires access to the isolated stems of a song in order to effectively produce a multi-

channel mix in formats such as 5.1 or 7.1. However, in scenarios where multichannel

output is desired but only the stereo mix is available, a blind upmix of the stereo

content becomes necessary. Without access to the original multitrack recordings or

stems, the process of blind audio upmixing from stereo to multichannel surround

remains a significant challenge. Traditional approaches to upmixing have included

manual mixing to a multichannel format, surround matrix decoding [11], and algo-

rithmic digital signal processing (DSP) techniques that aim to decompose the stereo

signal into separate components based on interchannel correlation [13–16].

1.2      Problem Identification and Statement

   Among the various approaches to upmixing, one of the most common in blind

scenarios is Primary-Ambient Extraction (PAE), which focuses on decomposing stereo

audio into primary and ambient signal components by analyzing time and frequency

correlations between the stereo channels. The processed output is then spatially

distributed across the surround channels. While effective, algorithmic methods such

as those proposed in [11, 13–16] still exhibit several limitations:


   • Lack of Generalization: Traditional methods rely on predefined assumptions

      about signal characteristics, making them less adaptable to diverse musical

      content.
                                                                                      3
   • Limited Context Awareness and Artifacts: Processing audio in short, in-

      dependent frames without considering long-term contextual dependencies often

      leads to incoherence and audible artifacts. This includes spectral and phase

      inconsistencies, as well as other distortions that manifest as perceptual discon-

      tinuities in the output.


   By framing the upmixing problem as a separation task, deep neural networks

(DNNs) offer a wide range of potential solutions by leveraging advanced techniques

such as autoencoder-based latent space representations, attention mechanisms, and

hybrid time-frequency processing. DNNs have already demonstrated strong efficacy

in the field of music source separation (MSS), successfully isolating musical stems with

high accuracy while preserving temporal and spectral coherence [1,3,9,17–29]. These

innovations enable models to efficiently extract complex audio features and capture

long-term dependencies, making them well-suited to learn the intricate characteristics

of decorrelated ambient signal components from stereo audio.



1.3      Research Question and Hypotheses

   While DNNs have been successfully applied in MSS and other complex audio

processing tasks, their potential for direct ambient signal extraction in the context

of surround upmixing remains largely unexplored. Existing DNN-based upmixing

approaches still exhibit several key limitations:


   • Lack of Research Focus: There is a notable gap in research and published

      work specifically addressing DNN-based PAE for upmixing. Most existing solu-
                                                                                      4
      tions continue to rely on traditional DSP techniques, with the majority of DNN

      applications focused on instrument and dialogue separation tasks [7, 8].


   • Simplistic Neural Architectures: Existing DNN-based upmixing approaches

      primarily employ basic feature extraction pipelines and shallow network designs,

      lacking the advanced separation capabilities demonstrated by state-of-the-art

      MSS models [10, 30, 31].


   Thus, the fundamental research question guiding this thesis is: How can advanced

MSS architectures be adapted for ambient signal extraction to enable a more effective

blind stereo-to-surround upmixing system?



1.4      Structure of the Thesis

   The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows:


   • Chapter 2: Literature Review – Provides an overview of MSS techniques

      and stereo-to-surround upmixing approaches, covering both classical DSP-based

      methods and recent developments in deep learning. Key architectures, datasets,

      and integration strategies relevant to PAE are reviewed in depth.


   • Chapter 3: Methodology – Describes the proposed solution, research mo-

      tivation, and experimental design. This includes system architecture design,

      dataset procurement and preparation (PAEDB), model selection and training

      strategy.


   • Chapter 4: Evaluation and Performance Metrics – Details the setup and

      execution of the evaluation process, including both the subjective listening study
                                                                                   5
  and objective metric assessments. Participant demographics, stimuli selection,

  study design and procedures, and metric computation methods are described.


• Chapter 5: Results – Presents and analyzes the experimental results. This

  includes model training curves, computational resource comparison, statistical

  analysis of subjective MUSHRA listening scores across multiple perceptual di-

  mensions, and objective performance results based on SDR, SI-SDR, and LSD

  metrics.


• Chapter 6: Conclusion – Summarizes the findings and contributions of the

  thesis, discusses the implications of the results, acknowledges limitations in data

  and methodology, and proposes directions for future research and improvement.
CHAPTER 2

Literature Review


2.1     Introduction to Music Source Separation

   Music Source Separation (MSS) is the process of decomposing a mixed audio

signal into its individual components, often referred to as “stems.” In most music

separation tasks, these stem sources typically include primary musical components

such as vocals, bass, drums, and other instrumental sources [27]. MSS has a broad

range of applications, from rearranging and remixing the original source mixture [32],

speech enhancement [33], vocal removal for instrumental tracks [20], and most relevant

to the thesis, audio upmixing [7,8]. These scenarios share a similar need for modifying

or removing specific elements from the target mixture track, which historically has

proven to be a challenging task due to the complex nature of overlapping frequencies

and highly correlated audio signals [14].

   Historically, source separation has been approached using classical DSP tech-

niques, such as independent component analysis (ICA) with blind source separation

(BSS) [33, 34], non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) [35], and generalized wiener

filtering (GWF) [36]. These methods laid the groundwork for the evolution of more

advanced deep learning approaches explored in Section 2.1.1.

2.1.1     Deep Learning Approaches for MSS

   Deep learning, a subset of machine learning (ML), focuses on developing deep

neural networks (DNN), which are computational models inspired by the human

brain and consist of multiple hidden layers of interconnected nodes (or “neurons”)

that process data. During training, the model learns complex patterns by adjusting

weights to minimize prediction errors, usually in the form of a unified loss function. A

separate validation dataset is used in tandem and helps the training environment tune

parameters to prevent overfitting. Finally, a smaller test dataset is used to evaluate

the model’s performance on new, unseen data, providing an unbiased measure of its

effectiveness and generalization.

   The rise of ML and deep learning in audio processing has advanced the field

of MSS, overcoming challenges inherent in the classical DSP methods mentioned

earlier. Deep learning models have the ability to learn complex temporal and spectral
                                                                                     8
representations of audio signals, enabling better separation performance, especially

in challenging scenarios such as overlapping frequencies that may occur in the source

stems [2, 9, 17, 18].

    The following subsections provide a brief overview of various DNN model archi-

tectures that have defined the state-of-the-art (SOTA) in MSS, organized by the type

of input audio domain they operate within and their unique contributions to the field.

It is important to note that the architectures introduced in this literature review is

not an exhaustive list of all researched and developed architectures, but rather serves

to provide an overview of the state of the field.


2.1.1.1    Spectrogram and Waveform Domain Models

    To introduce domain-specific processing, time-frequency domain approaches en-

compass models that transform the audio into the frequency domain, typically using

the short-time Fourier transform (STFT). Time-frequency approaches work on the

same principles of image recognition models by leveraging the spectral representations

of audio as spectrogram images, allowing models to better capture complex and over-

lapping spectral patterns over time frames. Models in this domain separate sources

by applying predicted frequency masks to spectrogram time frames [17, 24].

    While time-frequency approaches excel in capturing high-resolution spectral pat-

terns, they come with inherent trade-offs such as artifacts introduced from time-

frequency transformations, phase reconstruction errors, limited temporal precision,

and high computational costs. Time-domain models operate directly on raw wave-

forms, bypassing the need for spectrogram transformations.
                                                                                      9
   One of the earliest influential time-domain models is Wave-U-Net [26], which

adapts the U-Net architecture to one-dimensional waveform processing (see Sec-

tion 2.1.1.3). Wave-U-Net’s use of downsampling and upsampling blocks captures

features at different temporal scales directly from raw audio. By introducing lin-

ear interpolation for upsampling, it avoids aliasing artifacts and achieves competitive

separation quality, laying foundational work for more complex models.

   Demucs [18] further enhances the time-domain approach for music separation by

adopting the U-Net encoder-decoder structure but introduces a bidirectional LSTM

between the encoder and decoder that facilitates multi-scale feature learning for longer

sequences of audio.


2.1.1.2   Hybrid Time- and Frequency-Domain Models

   Hybrid models have been introduced with the aim to combine the advantages of

both time-domain and frequency-domain approaches. Improving from time-domain

Demucs, Hybrid Demucs [19] keeps the same U-Net architecture but includes an

additional processing path in the frequency-domain. By optimizing over the signal’s

raw waveform and spectrogram image, this approach has been shown to enhance

separation performance for complex audio signals by leveraging the strengths of both

domains in order to capture detailed temporal and spectral information.

2.1.1.3   Autoencoders and U-Net Architectures

   The autoencoder is a popular architecture that consists of convolutional encoder

and decoder blocks that aim to reduce the input data into a lower-dimensional rep-

resentation, to then reconstruct the data back into its original dimension from these

learned representations. In audio applications, this involves compressing complex

waveforms and spectrograms to a lower-dimensional feature space, where meaningful

temporal and spectral patterns are extracted, and then reconstructing the audio to

its original resolution while optimizing it for a specific task.
                                                                                                11




Figure 2.3: Diagram of the U-Net architecture, illustrating its encoder-decoder structure with skip

connections [2].



    A notable extension of this is the U-Net architecture, first introduced by [2] for

biomedical image segmentation, which builds on the autoencoder structure by incor-

porating skip connections between the encoder and decoder blocks. These connections

help preserve high-resolution features during reconstruction, improving the model’s

ability to retain fine details and spatial coherence.

    The success of autoencoder-like architectures in image processing has also trans-

lated well to audio spectrograms, making them a popular choice for audio related

tasks due to their ability to retain contextual and fine-grained details. Many MSS

models expand on this foundational architecture, integrating enhancements such as

hybrid domain processing [19], attention mechanisms [1, 4, 9], and additional latent

space conditioning [37] to improve separation performance.

                    Figure 2.4: Diagram of the band-split module in [3].



2.1.1.4   Recurrent Neural Network Models

   While many of the models mentioned above rely on convolutional layers, U-Net

architectures, or a combination of both, some architectures have turned to recurrent

neural networks (RNNs) to better handle the challenge of processing longer sequences

of audio data. Dual-path RNNs (DPRNN) have been proposed as an alternative to

conventional 1-D CNN layers in TasNet to tackle the challenge of long sequence

modeling by chunking input sequences and processing intra- and inter-chunk data

separately [25]. This architecture enhances computational efficiency while preserving

high-quality separation in the time domain.


2.1.1.5   Band-Split and Multi-Band Spectrogram-Based Models

   There is a recent emergence of frequency band-splitting techniques that outper-

forms the previous state-of-the-art models. The band-split RNN (BSRNN), intro-

duces a novel frequency-domain method by splitting the spectrogram into subbands

and using a stacked residual RNN to process both intra- and cross-band dependencies,

achieving fine-grained frequency resolution and improving separation performance for

complex music signals [3].

                  Figure 2.5: The Transformer model architecture from [4].



2.1.1.6   Attention Mechanisms

   Attention mechanisms in machine learning enable models to dynamically focus on

the most relevant parts of input data, improving their ability to capture dependencies

across long sequences. Originally introduced in natural language processing (NLP),

attention plays a crucial role in handling sequential data, where input text is first

tokenized into discrete units before processing. The Transformer is a sequence-based

model that incorporates self-attention and positional encoding mechanisms to main-

tain long-range coherence in generation tasks in NLP [4] and image generation [38].

   This concept extends to audio, where waveforms or spectrograms can be framed

into smaller segments that act as tokens for the transformer-based attention layers to
                                                                                  14
maintain long-range context in music generation [39], and more recently, MSS tasks.

Models like Hybrid Transformer Demucs (HTDemucs) and Latent Source Attentive

Frequency Transformation (LaSAFT) integrate transformer layers into their archi-

tectural structures, replacing the biLSTM traditionally used for sequential data pro-

cessing [1, 17]. By incorporating attention mechanisms, transformer-based attention

mechanisms significantly outperform other architectures in capturing and maintaining

long-term contextual information.

   The Band-Split Rotary Position Embedding Transformer (BS-RoFormer), further

demonstrates the efficacy of Transformers in sequential data modeling by combining

them with band-split modules for more accurate and efficient handling of spectral

data in MSS [9].



2.1.2    Open Source Datasets for MSS

   The development and success of MSS models heavily rely on large-scale datasets

like MUSDB18 [40], widely considered the benchmark for evaluating model perfor-

mance. MUSDB18 contains 150 full-length tracks, totaling approximately 10 hours

of audio across genres, with ground-truth stems for four categories: vocals, drums,

bass, and other. The dataset is divided into 100 training tracks and 50 test tracks,

all in stereo and encoded at a 44.1kHz sampling rate. An uncompressed alternative,

MUSDB18-HQ [41], is also available for models targeting wider bandwidth audio,

offering WAV files that extend to 22kHz content while MUSDB18 only extends to

16kHz. Due to these ground-truth classes, most models trained on MUSDB18 pro-

duce the same four separated outputs.
                                                                                     15
   While other datasets, such as MoisesDB [42], expand on these options by including

240 tracks across 45 artists and twelve musical genres with more granular source

categories (e.g., five and six stems), the limited availability of large, open-source

datasets remains a significant challenge in the field of MSS.

   The scarcity of open-source datasets remains a major pain point in the MSS field

of hindered standardization and slow academic progress. Much of the innovation in

source separation is currently driven by private companies with access to proprietary

multitrack data, leading to models that are often not reproducible. For example,

HTDemucs was trained on an additional 800 songs beyond the MUSDB18 dataset [1],

and Spleeter leveraged over 20,000 tracks for training [43]. Furthermore, the legality

of data ownership in many of these cases remains unclear, raising ethical and legal

concerns about the use and distribution of these pre-trained models.



2.2     Automatic Stereo-to-Multichannel Upmixing

2.2.1     Overview of Multichannel Surround Sound

   Multichannel audio configurations, such as 5.1 and 7.1, are designed to create en-

veloping audio experiences by extending beyond regular stereo audio. These formats

are commonly used in home theaters, cinemas, and gaming environments to recre-

ate spatial soundscapes by distributing audio across multiple channels, including left,

right, center, low-frequency effects (LFE), and surround channels [12]. The accurate

reproduction of these channels is essential for providing listeners with a realistic and

immersive auditory experience.
                                                                                    16
2.2.2    Definition of Upmixing

   In the context of audio and music production, upmixing refers to the process of

converting an audio signal from its original format to a larger configuration with more

channels [44]. Conversely, converting an audio signal from a larger format to a smaller

one with fewer channels is known as downmixing. Upmixing can be categorized into

“informed” upmixing, which uses prior information or metadata to guide the con-

struction of the upmixed output [45], and “blind” upmixing which aims to create the

output purely from the input signal without the use of side information [44].



2.2.3    Available Solutions on the Consumer Market

   There already exists surround upmixing solutions available on the market that

are capable of converting stereo content to surround sound. These solutions include

consumer electronics such as audio/video receivers (AVRs) integrated with Dolby Pro

Logic II [11] designed for home theater setups, and software plugins [46] for use in

digital audio workstations (DAWs).



2.2.4    Classical DSP Approaches to Stereo Upmixing

   Proposed methods for upmixing stereo audio to multichannel formats have in-

cluded techniques such as the “passive” and “adaptive” surround decoding matrix [11],

primary-ambient extraction (PAE) [10,16,47], mid-side (M-S) decomposition [48], and

center channel derivation [6].

2.2.4.1   Dolby Surround Decoding

   Dolby Surround Decoding is based on a predefined processing matrix, where ad-

ditional surround channels are derived from a standard stereo signal through a com-

bination of panning, phase manipulation, difference signals, and filtering techniques.

There are two primary decoding methods: passive and adaptive. Passive decoding

simply applies a fixed matrix to extract surround information as it exists in the stereo

mix, without altering the levels dynamically. Adaptive decoding, on the other hand,

employs gain riding and signal cancellation mechanisms to resolve a dominance vec-

tor, which determines the most prominent directional component of the sound field

for playback. This helps improve separation between channels and enhances spatial

accuracy. Dolby Pro Logic II (DPLII) extends the original Dolby Surround Decoding

method by using a refined matrixing approach that improves channel separation and

stereo width in the surround field. As shown in Table 2.1, the DPLII matrix applies

phase shifts of +90° (j) and -90° (k) to generate the surround channel information [11].


2.2.4.2   Primary-Ambient Extraction

   Primary-Ambient Extraction (PAE) is a popular technique used in upmixing that

operates on the assumption that a stereo signal model is comprised of primary and
                                                                                   18
ambient components. The primary components of a signal consist of direct and

correlated sounds that are prominently shared between stereo channels. This includes

primary instrument stems and prominent audio signals such as vocals, drums, and

bass. In contrast, the ambient component is comprised of diffuse and decorrelated

signals, such as reverb and delay, which are less common between the two stereo

channels.

   PAE has emerged as a preferred approach for upmixing because it effectively al-

lows for the expansion of the perceived spatial field of stereo audio by routing the

ambient signal to the additional surround channels of a multichannel surround sys-

tem. In a 5.1 surround speaker configuration, this would be the rear left and right

speakers. Various techniques have been developed to achieve this decomposition

of direct and diffuse signals, including methods based on frequency-domain inter-

channel coherence [13], cross- and auto-correlation [14], principal component analysis

(PCA) [15,16], frequency-domain normalized least-mean-square (NLMS) adaptive fil-

tering, and time-domain least-mean-square (LMS) filtering [8, 49].

2.2.4.3    Center Channel Derivation

    Center Channel Derivation or Decomposition (CCD) leverages the psychoacous-

tic phenomenon of the ”phantom center,” where sounds located in the center of a

stereo image are perceived as originating in-between the physical stereo speakers [6].

CCD extends the PAE stereo signal model and works off of the assumption that the

primary-ambient signal components are distributed amongst the three front chan-

nels, where the center channel contains only the primary signal, and the left and

right channels contain a mixture of the corresponding channels primary and ambi-

ent components (see Figure 2.7). With an adapted three-channel primary-ambient

signal model, the CCD algorithm applies vector-based signal decomposition in the

frequency domain to geometrically estimate a dedicated center channel. This ded-

icated center channel mitigates acoustic cross-talk issues inherent in the phantom

center effect, where identical sounds from both speakers create phase cancellations

that can introduce comb-filtering in stereo audio playback. By separating the cen-

ter into a physical channel, CCD reduces cross-talk and improves audio clarity and
                                                                                    20
dialogue intelligibility in multichannel audio applications [50]. This algorithm is not

only effective for generating the front channels in a multichannel surround format,

but may also enhance ambient extraction quality for the surround channels in a 5.1

upmix by serving as a preprocessing step to remove correlated primary components

prior to ambient signal extraction (see Section 3.2.1).



2.2.5    Deep Learning Approaches to Upmixing

   The use of DNNs for upmixing has been explored in prior work that focuses

primarily on achieving PAE through neural networks and applies additional post-

processing to assist in panning and distribution of the signal appropriately [10,30,31,

44].

   One method uses a supervised learning approach to extract ambient components

from mono recordings, modeling primary-ambient separation as a classification prob-

lem over frequency bins [44]. A similar DNN-based approach classifies frequency-

frame bins as either direct or ambient sound using a fully-connected multi-layer

perceptron (MLP) [10]. Another method trains a network on two-to-five channel

surround decoding, optimizing spectral weights to approximate spatial properties of

surround sound for enhanced performance over traditional methods [30]. Another

similar approach implements subband-based upmixing to learn the features of the

center and rear surround channels from the multichannel input [31].

   While these methods focus on blind upmixing through PAE, a unique approach

leverages a variational autoencoder (VAE) to learn the spatial distribution of musical

instruments in a source 5.1 mix, mapping these locations in a latent space [37]. This

model then upmixes the target input stereo audio through style transfer, applying
                                                                                     21
the learned spatial locations of instruments onto the stereo input through directional

panning. Although not directly performing source separation task, this paper still

suggests the possibility for ML architectures to possess the ability to capture and

learn complex spatial features of audio in the context of upmixing.



2.2.6    Voice-Primary-Ambient Extraction Upmixing

   With recent advancements in MSS, emerging research has begun to integrate MSS

architectures (discussed in Section 2.1.1) into upmixing frameworks. In the context of

stereo-to-5.1 upmixing, Voice-Primary-Ambient Extraction (VPA) assigns the output

of a pretrained MSS model as the primary component for the front channels, while

some form of ambient signal extraction is used to generate the surround channels. The

VPA method was first proposed by Samsung Research, using the OpenUnmix vocal

extraction model [27] to isolate vocals in the center channel, assign residual stems to

the left and right channels, and apply the Equal-Levels Ambience Extraction (ELAE)

algorithm [14] to generate ambient signals for the rear channels (see Figure 2.8).

      input signal to separate the left, center, and right components of the stereo

  II. Ambient Extraction Block

     spatial separation between the front and rear channels and to mitigate potential

 III. Additional Filtering Blocks

     surround channels. This is achieved using a low-pass biquad filter with a cutoff

     frequency of 200 Hz for the LFE channel, and a high-pass biquad filter with a

3.2.4      Model Training Parameters

    Our training procedure follows a supervised learning training strategy over the pre-

pared PAEDB audio dataset. For simplicity and initial experimentation, the training

parameters are set as follows, using the same configurations reported in the respective

literature for each model. See Section 5.1 for the results of the training runs.

   1. Mel-RoFormer – The optimizer used is AdamW, with a learning rate of 5e-5

       and an EMA momentum of 0.999 for stabilizing parameter updates. Training

       employs a batch size of 3 (effectively doubled to 6 with the dual-GPU setup),

       with gradient accumulation steps set to 1 and mixed precision enabled for im-

       proved computational efficiency. The training run is set for 100 epochs for 4,000

       steps, with a learning rate reduction applied after 2 epochs of no improvement

       in the monitored metric, using a reduction factor of 0.95 to fine-tune the opti-

       mization and prevent overfitting.
                                                                                   31
  2. 3-Layer MLP – The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer with a

     learning rate of 1e-3, batch size of 16, and 200 training epochs. Mixed precision

     training is enabled, and learning rate reduction is applied with a patience of

     5 epochs and a reduction factor of 0.95. The network comprises three linear

     layers with ReLU activations, reducing the feature dimensionality through hid-

     den layers of sizes 15 and 10 before outputting a single probability through a

     sigmoid activation, representing the presence of ambient content.


   The loss function for training the Mel-RoFormer model combines L1 loss for

waveform reconstruction in the time domain with a weighted multi-resolution STFT

(MRSTFT) loss to maintain fidelity across multiple time-frequency resolutions, fol-

lowing the approach in [9]. In contrast, the MLP model uses only L1 loss applied

to the final softmax layer output, with binary labels assigned as 0 for primary stems

and 1 for ambient stems, consistent with the implementation in [10].

   Data augmentation is applied through random loudness scaling between 0.1 and

1.0 to improve generalization. Additionally, multi-threaded data loading is enabled

using eight parallel processes to optimize training performance.

4.3      Objective Metric Evaluation

   In addition to the subjective listening study, objective metrics were used to assess

the separation and reconstruction quality of the upmixed audio. These evaluations

are essential for quantifying signal quality and comparing the performance of different

upmixing systems.

   For this analysis, we focus on comparing System C (Mel-RoFormer) and System

D (MLP), using the DSP-generated signals as reference ground truths. Since the

PAEDB dataset was constructed using ELAE [14] (as described in Section 3.2), these

reference signals serve as the baseline for evaluating how accurately each neural net-

work reproduces the target ambient signal. Thus, the following objective metrics are

used to evaluate system performance:


   • Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SDR): Measures the overall quality of the sep-

      arated signals by computing the ratio of target signal energy to distortion,

      including noise, interference, and artifacts. Higher SDR values indicate better

      separation quality.


   • Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR): A variant of SDR

      that eliminates dependencies on absolute signal amplitude, providing a more

      robust evaluation of separation quality by focusing on distortions relative to

      the target signal.
                                                                                  39
   • Log-Spectral Distance (LSD): Assesses spectral fidelity by computing the

     logarithmic distance between the estimated and ground-truth power spectra.

     Lower LSD values indicate more accurate spectral reconstruction.



4.3.1    Metrics Processing Procedure

   To automate the objective evaluation process, a Python script was developed to

iterate over each corresponding pair of reference and estimated audio files. For each

excerpt, metrics were computed using 45-second analysis windows with a 5-second

hop size, producing a sequence of frame-wise evaluations across the duration of each

track.

   For each excerpt, metric scores were averaged across all analysis windows to yield

a per-track result. An overall mean was then computed across the full 12-track

evaluation set to produce a system-level performance metric.

Conclusion

   To summarize, this thesis investigated the application of DNNs as a solution

for PAE and addressing the limitations of previous DSP algorithms in the context

of stereo-to-surround audio upmixing. By reframing ambient signal extraction as a

source separation task, we aimed to develop a more flexible and effective approach

by utilizing high-complexity MSS architectures presented in recent literature.

   The previous chapters provided a comprehensive review of the SOTA systems

and techniques in the fields of MSS and automatic blind upmixing, identified key

knowledge gaps in the literature, and outlined the guiding research question and pro-

posed methodologies that address the identified research gap. We detailed the design

and implementation of the data procurement and preparation of the custom PAEDB

dataset, the supervised learning strategy and training parameters, and the selected

model architecture for PAE optimization, integrated into the extraction blocks of

the VPA upmixing system. This was followed by a thorough evaluation through a

designed participant listening study and objective performance metrics.

   The experimental results demonstrate that the DNN-based system consistently

outperformed the MLP model in both training efficiency and separation quality. The

training loss curves showed that the DNN model achieved stable convergence, while

                                         57
                                                                                      58
the MLP exhibited multiple plateaus and reached an earlier performance ceiling,

indicating the architecture’s limited capacity for learning complex ambient extraction

patterns.

   In terms of computational efficiency, the MLP model trained faster with lower

GPU utilization, while the DNN model required significantly more time and resources

despite employing data parallelization across multiple GPUs.

   The subjective evaluation results revealed significant differences in listener ratings

across systems and perceptual attributes. Non-parametric Friedman tests showed

that the DNN-based system (System C) and the DSP-based system (System B) not

only outperformed the other systems, but also closely matched each other in overall

perceptual quality across stimuli. In contrast, Spectral Quality ratings highlighted

a consistent disadvantage for the MLP-based system (System D), which was rated

significantly lower than the others. Participant feedback attributed this to audible

artifacts introduced by the MLP’s gating mechanism for ambient content. Interest-

ingly, some listeners noted that these same artifacts occasionally enhanced perceived

immersion, suggesting a complex interplay between spectral fidelity and subjective

engagement depending on the listening context.

   The objective evaluation confirmed that SDR differences indicated a modest im-

provement in signal reconstruction quality, with SI-SDR showing a significant advan-

tage for the DNN model in reducing distortion, while LSD revealed greater spectral

mismatch for the MLP model, further supporting the method’s limited coherence in

ambient reconstruction.

   Overall, these results demonstrate that the proposed DNN-based approach, specif-

ically the Mel-RoFormer architecture trained from scratch, effectively learns the com-
                                                                                    59
plex structure of DSP-generated ambient signals. Both subjective and objective eval-

uations suggest that this model delivers perceptually coherent and high-quality ambi-

ent signal extraction. Notably, the Mel-RoFormer closely matches the performance of

the traditional DSP algorithm (ELAE method) while maintaining the flexibility and

efficiency of a learned neural architecture. Compared to the simpler MLP method

proposed in [10], the more complex DNN approach provides substantial improvements

in both perceived spatial quality and spectral fidelity, demonstrating the potential of

deep, attention-based architectures for high-fidelity ambience extraction in the con-

text of surround audio upmixing.



6.1     Discussion of Limitations

6.1.1     Observation of Objective Metrics and Loss Functions

   Although we observed measurable improvements in the DNN model’s performance

across SDR, SI-SDR, and LSD, the absolute values of these metrics remain on the

lower end compared to SOTA MSS models optimized for the music separation task.

This suggests an inherent limitation in relying solely on conventional signal recon-

struction metrics for evaluating ambient signal quality. Ambient components are,

by nature, diffuse, decorrelated, and often stochastic, making them fundamentally

different from structured sources like vocals or drums. To this effect, models trained

to optimize over SDR or SI-SDR metrics may focus on minimizing frame-level recon-

struction error rather than capturing the perceptual and spatial characteristics that

define realistic ambience.
                                                                                    60
   Similarly, the chosen L1 and MRSTFT losses may also struggle to guide the model

toward perceptually meaningful solutions in the context of ambient extraction, as they

do not explicitly account for stereo image coherence or spatial diffusion.

   This mismatch between the nature of the task and the evaluation criteria may

lead to suboptimal learning during training, despite achieving a satisfactory percep-

tual outcome. If the objective function fails to align with perceptual relevance, the

network may converge on solutions that technically satisfy the loss function but fail

to produce perceptually convincing results. This could explain why prior work on

ambient extraction has consistently reported poor SDR values, often in the negative

range [30, 31]. To put it simply, even if the generated ambient signals sound spatially

diffuse and perceptually coherent, they do not necessarily align frame-for-frame with

the reference signals, leading to penalization under standard objective metrics.



6.1.2     Data Scarcity and Subsequent Performance Ceiling

   Another key limitation identified during this investigation was the scarcity of

publicly available datasets for primary ambient signal extraction. While datasets

for speech dereverberation and enhancement exist, they are typically constrained

to speech and residual components, lacking the dry and reverberant music content

required for broader music applications. As a result, we were unable to leverage

large-scale, ground-truth datasets tailored specifically for ambient signal separation.

To address this, we relied on DSP-based ambient extraction methods [14] to generate

the training targets.

   These algorithmically generated signals may inherently introduce a performance

ceiling, as the model can only learn what the DSP algorithm is capable of producing.
                                                                                     61
Rather than mitigating the spectral and temporal artifacts introduced by traditional

DSP methods, the model risks replicating these very artifacts. One could argue that

training a DNN to mimic a DSP-based ambient extraction algorithm allows it to

generalize and internalize the rule-based logic within its layers, potentially providing

a strong foundation for future fine-tuning. Nonetheless, the additional computational

and resource costs required for this approach must be carefully justified if the learned

model continues to inherit the limitations and artifacts of the DSP algorithm it was

trained to emulate.



6.2     Future Work

   These observations underscore the need for alternative evaluation and training

strategies in the context of ambient extraction. Future work should explore objective

metrics that better correlate with spatial perception, such as measures of decorrelation

and diffuseness to more accurately assess ambient signal quality. Without appropriate

metrics, training objectives may fail to capture the nuanced perceptual attributes that

listeners associate with high-quality upmixed ambience.

   In parallel, it is important to move beyond standard L1 and MRSTFT losses,

which primarily penalize waveform and spectral reconstruction errors, and consider

custom loss functions that also better reflect the perceptual properties of ambient

audio. Potential directions include losses based on mid/side processing, inter-channel

correlation, or phase coherence, which could guide the model toward learning spatially

rich and perceptually convincing ambient representations.

   Another future directive would be the procurement and development of a dataset

containing dry and wet music recordings specifically tailored for music dereverber-
                                                                                    62
ation and ambient effect extraction. Such a dataset would enable models to learn

directly from real ambient signals, rather than being constrained by DSP-generated

training data. This would address one of the key limitations identified in this study’s

evaluation, where reliance on algorithmically approximated targets introduced a per-

formance ceiling and limited the perceptual realism of the extracted ambience.

   Finally, the model architecture and feature space of current MSS models may not

be inherently optimized for ambient extraction. Future research could explore the

development of custom architectures explicitly designed for this task. One promising

direction is the integration of latent space representations inspired by variational

autoencoders (VAEs), as demonstrated in style-transfer upmixing methods [37], which

have shown the ability to capture spatial instrument placement across channels. Such

representations may provide a more suitable feature space for modeling the diffuse

and spatially distributed nature of ambient signals.

   While this study demonstrated the potential of DNN-based PAE for stereo-to-

surround upmixing, several areas warrant further investigation to refine and advance

the approach. Addressing these challenges may lead to more effective ambient ex-

traction techniques, ultimately improving the perceptual quality and spatial realism

of stereo-to-surround upmixing systems.
